  - label: RULES

lessons_learned:
  - Pack light in the canoe. Ours was so loaded it steered like a barge, and fast water shows no mercy to a barge.
  - There's no prize for running a rapid. Walking the canoe around the flip-machine rapid was the best decision of the trip — we watched more than twenty boats go over while we ate a snack.
  - Dry-bag everything, then dry-bag it again. A full day of rain will find every zipper you left open.

## The route

The Buffalo was America's first national river, and the Ponca to Pruitt
stretch is its greatest hits reel: about 23 river miles through the Ponca
Wilderness, where limestone bluffs rise a few hundred feet straight out of
the water around seemingly every bend. On paper it's easy paddling — Class I
riffles and long calm pools. On a rain-swollen late-May weekend, with two
people who had never done a canoe-and-kayak trip before, the paper
undersold it.

## Day 1 — rain, fast water, and one angry tree

We put in at the Ponca low-water bridge with a canoe and a kayak rented from
**Buffalo Outdoor Center**, under a rain that started early and simply never
stopped. The river came up with it — high, quick, and pushier than either of
us expected for a "beginner" float.

Here's what nobody tells you about your first canoe trip: a heavily packed
canoe in fast water steers like a shopping cart with a mind of its own. We
learned that the hard way when the current won an argument and slammed the
canoe into a tree hard enough to break a piece off the hull. The canoe,
to its credit, shrugged it off and stayed fully functional — which is more
than we could say for our nerves. From there we learned the river on the
river: reading the current, setting up turns early, respecting what the
water wanted to do.

The scenery made the chaos worth it. **Roark Bluff** runs along the water
near Steel Creek like a wall someone forgot to end, and downstream the river
slides beneath **Big Bluff**, the tallest face between the Rockies and the
Appalachians. Then came the rapid. One particular drop near Kyles was
flipping boats all afternoon — we counted more than twenty canoes go over
while we watched. We beached, walked the canoe around, and kept our gear
dry while the river collected everyone else's coolers. Finding a campsite
took some hunting — the gravel bars near Kyles were crowded — but we ended
up with a secluded bar under a bluff, all to ourselves.

## Day 2 — the river makes it up to you

Day 2 started gray and damp, and then the Ozarks did their thing: by late
morning the sky broke open and the river turned friendly. This is the quiet
stretch past **Erbie** where the crowds thin, and we finally got the float
we'd imagined — long pools, easy current, spaghetti for lunch on a gravel
bar, and a cooler of Coronas with fresh limes doing steady work as we
drifted.

We swam, waded, and took zero-hurry breaks until we found another secluded
gravel bar for the night. Dinner was the trip's peak: tacos with homemade
pico cooked over the campfire, eaten with a bluff glowing across the water.
Type-one fun, start to finish.

## Day 3 — glass to Pruitt

The last morning was the kind you plan trips around: dead-calm water holding
a perfect reflection of the bluffs, coffee from the hammock, and a few
unhurried miles into the **Pruitt** landing. Best part: Buffalo Outdoor
Center shuttles your car to the takeout while you float, so ours was sitting
there waiting — load the boats, hand them off, drive home.

## Logistics

We rented the canoe and kayak from Buffalo Outdoor Center in Ponca, and
their car-shuttle service is the move — no doubling back, no waiting on a
bus. No permits are needed to float or camp on gravel bars; just follow the
rules above. Watch the USGS Ponca gauge when planning: this upper stretch is
rain-fed and seasonal, and the same trip in August would be a boat drag. The
common two-day split is Ponca to Kyles (10.7 miles) then Kyles to Pruitt
(13.2) — our three-day version left room for the river's best feature, which
is not being in a hurry.
